# The Large Ears of Śrī Gaṇeśa

The large ears of Śrī Gaṇeśa are among the most immediately recognisable features of His sacred form.

They are also among the most direct teachings.

Before wisdom can be expressed, something must first be received.

Before a wise decision can be made, something must first be understood.

Before another person's experience can be met with compassion, it must first be heard.

The large ears therefore invite us into a discipline that is deceptively simple and extraordinarily difficult:

**learn to listen.**

Not merely to hear sounds.

Not merely to wait for our turn to speak.

But to become genuinely present to what is being offered.

## The Wisdom of Listening

In many Indian philosophical and spiritual traditions, ***śravaṇam*** — attentive hearing or listening — occupies a foundational place in the journey towards knowledge.

A teaching must first be heard.

That hearing must then be examined and reflected upon.

With deeper contemplation, understanding can gradually become lived insight.

The familiar sequence of ***śravaṇam, mananam, nididhyāsanam*** expresses this movement beautifully.

First, receive.

Then, reflect.

Then, internalise.

The large ears of Śrī Gaṇeśa offer a visual reminder of that first essential movement.

**Wisdom begins with receptivity.**

A mind that refuses to receive cannot grow.

A person who believes that they already know everything leaves little room for transformation.

Listening is therefore not simply a communication skill.

It is an attitude towards knowledge itself.

## Why Are the Ears So Large?

Sacred iconography often communicates through emphasis.

A feature may be enlarged because the quality associated with it deserves attention.

Gaṇeśa's ears are deliberately prominent.

They invite the observer to notice them.

And once noticed, they invite a question:

**What should I be listening to?**

The answer is larger than ordinary conversation.

We need to listen to other people.

We need to listen to the world around us.

We need to listen to experience.

We need to listen to what our own reactions are revealing.

And, within spiritual practice, we need to become quiet enough to listen for that which cannot be heard through ordinary noise.

The ears therefore become symbols of **receptive intelligence**.

## Hearing Is Not the Same as Listening

A person can hear every word and understand almost nothing.

Listening requires participation.

It requires attention.

It requires patience.

It requires restraint.

It requires the willingness to remain with another person's meaning long enough for that meaning to become clear.

This distinction is particularly important in an age of constant distraction.

A conversation may take place while attention is divided between a phone, a screen, unfinished work and an internal argument.

The words are heard.

The person is not.

Gaṇeśa's large ears suggest a different standard.

**Be present enough to receive what is actually being said.**

## The Discipline of Not Responding Too Quickly

One of the great enemies of listening is the urge to respond immediately.

We begin preparing our answer while the other person is still speaking.

We anticipate their conclusion.

We classify their opinion.

We decide whether we agree or disagree.

And, in doing so, we stop listening.

Patience interrupts this habit.

It allows another person to finish.

It allows meaning to emerge.

It creates a small but powerful space between hearing and responding.

That space is where discernment begins.

The large ears therefore teach not only listening but **the patience required for listening to become understanding**.

## Śravaṇam — Receiving Before Interpreting

The spiritual significance of ***śravaṇam*** is especially important because genuine hearing precedes genuine reflection.

If the mind distorts what it hears from the beginning, later reasoning is built upon a false foundation.

This principle applies far beyond scripture.

It applies to everyday life.

A colleague says something difficult.

A family member expresses disappointment.

A student asks a question.

A friend shares a concern.

A leader receives uncomfortable feedback.

In each case, the quality of the first listening matters.

Did we actually hear?

Or did we immediately filter the words through our assumptions?

The ears of Gaṇeśa invite us to become more conscious of that distinction.

## Listening Beyond Words

Human communication is rarely contained entirely within sentences.

Tone matters.

Silence matters.

Pacing matters.

Emotion matters.

Context matters.

Sometimes the most important thing a person communicates is not explicitly stated.

Deep listening therefore involves attention to the whole communication.

What is being said?

What is being avoided?

What emotion is present?

What does the situation require?

What might the person need before they are ready for advice?

This does not mean imagining hidden meanings everywhere.

It means being attentive enough not to reduce communication to words alone.

## The Elephant's Attentiveness

The elephant provides a natural symbolic foundation for this teaching.

Its ears are large and highly visible, making them an obvious feature through which human beings can contemplate attentive hearing.

In the sacred imagination, this becomes more than an observation about anatomy.

The elephant-headed Gaṇeśa becomes an image of a being whose awareness is spacious rather than hurried.

The symbolic lesson is not that we must reproduce an animal's sensory abilities.

It is that we should cultivate a similar **quality of attentiveness**:

receptive,

steady,

unhurried,

and responsive to what is actually present.

## Patience Is Not Passivity

Patience is often misunderstood.

It can appear to mean waiting without acting.

But wise patience is different.

It is active.

It is deliberate.

It creates the conditions for better judgment.

To be patient is not to avoid responsibility.

It is to resist allowing impatience to make the decision for you.

A patient listener may still disagree.

A patient leader may still act decisively.

A patient parent may still establish boundaries.

A patient professional may still make difficult choices.

The difference is that action emerges after understanding rather than before it.

## The Modern Crisis of Attention

Contemporary life places enormous demands on attention.

Notifications arrive constantly.

Information competes for visibility.

Conversations are fragmented.

Opinions are broadcast continuously.

Many environments reward speed and reaction.

In such a culture, listening becomes increasingly difficult precisely because it requires something the environment does not naturally provide:

**undivided attention.**

Gaṇeśa's large ears can therefore be contemplated as a counter-image to distraction.

They remind us to enlarge our capacity to receive rather than endlessly enlarging our capacity to broadcast.

## The Courage to Hear What We Do Not Like

Listening is easiest when we hear something that confirms us.

The real test comes when another person challenges us.

Feedback can be uncomfortable.

Criticism can activate defensiveness.

A different worldview can threaten assumptions we have carried for years.

Yet intellectual growth often begins at exactly this point.

The wise listener does not automatically accept every argument.

Nor does the wise listener reject everything that causes discomfort.

Instead, the listener asks:

**Is there something here that I need to learn?**

This is intellectual humility in practice.

## Listening and Empathy

Empathy begins with attention.

Before we can genuinely appreciate another person's experience, we must give that experience room to be expressed.

Listening does not require agreement.

It does not require abandoning one's own position.

It requires recognising that another person is a complete human being whose experience deserves to be understood before it is evaluated.

That is why deep listening can be such a profound act of respect.

It says:

**You are worth my attention.**

## Listening Within the Family

Family life offers some of the most important opportunities to practise this teaching.

Because familiarity is so great within families, listening can become surprisingly difficult.

We think we already know what the other person is going to say.

We know their habits.

We know their reactions.

We know their history.

And sometimes that knowledge prevents us from hearing who they are becoming now.

Children change.

Parents change.

Partners change.

Family circumstances change.

Deep listening keeps relationships alive by allowing people to be encountered again.

## Listening in Friendship

Friendship also depends upon a willingness to listen beyond convenience.

Sometimes a friend does not need a solution.

They need to be heard.

Sometimes advice is useful.

Sometimes presence is more valuable.

The ability to distinguish between these moments is itself a form of wisdom.

A good friend does not merely provide answers.

A good friend creates enough space for another person to discover what they already know but have not yet been able to say.

## Listening in Society

At the social level, listening becomes even more demanding.

People carry different histories, cultures, beliefs, priorities and experiences.

Disagreement is inevitable.

The temptation is to listen only until we find something we can challenge.

That is not genuine listening.

Bridge-building begins when the intention changes.

Instead of:

**How do I defeat this argument?**

we ask:

**What is this person actually trying to communicate?**

Understanding another perspective does not require agreement.

It creates the possibility of dialogue without requiring sameness.

## Listening in Leadership

Leadership is often associated with giving direction.

But wise leadership begins with receiving information.

A leader who listens carefully learns what is actually happening rather than what people think the leader wants to hear.

People become more willing to surface problems.

Weak signals are noticed earlier.

Different perspectives enter the decision process.

Trust grows.

The large ears of Gaṇeśa therefore suggest a simple leadership principle:

**The quality of a leader's decisions is often limited by the quality of that leader's listening.**

## Listening in Professional Life

The same principle applies to teams and organisations.

Good meetings do not depend only on articulate speakers.

They also depend on attentive listeners.

A colleague who listens carefully can identify an overlooked dependency.

A manager who listens can detect a problem before it becomes a crisis.

A consultant who listens deeply can understand the real problem rather than solving the problem that was first described.

A designer who listens can discover the need beneath the request.

A mentor who listens can help another person find clarity rather than prescribing an answer too quickly.

Listening is therefore not an ornamental interpersonal skill.

It is a practical capability.

## The Discipline of Reflective Listening

One useful way to practise Gaṇeśa's teaching is to introduce a simple sequence:

**Listen → Reflect → Clarify → Respond**

First, listen without interruption.

Then reflect upon what you have heard.

If something is unclear, clarify rather than assume.

Only then respond.

This sequence is slower than reacting.

It is also often wiser.

The objective is not to turn every conversation into a formal procedure.

It is to create a habit of awareness that gradually becomes natural.

## Listening to the Inner Life

The large ears can also be turned inward.

We are accustomed to listening outwardly while neglecting our own inner state.

Yet the mind is constantly communicating.

Restlessness may be saying something.

Repeated irritation may be saying something.

Persistent avoidance may be saying something.

A recurring fear may be saying something.

An unexplained sense of dissatisfaction may be pointing towards something that deserves attention.

Inner listening does not mean believing every thought.

It means becoming sufficiently quiet to notice what is happening before automatically reacting to it.

## Silence as the Other Half of Listening

Listening requires silence.

Not necessarily complete outer silence, but enough inner quiet to receive.

Without silence, even meaningful words can pass through us without leaving an impression.

This is why periods of stillness can be valuable.

A few minutes without incoming information.

A walk without constant audio.

A quiet pause after prayer.

A moment before responding to difficult news.

Silence creates room.

And room allows attention to deepen.

## Spiritual Listening

In spiritual practice, listening can become even more subtle.

Prayer is often imagined as speaking towards the divine.

But devotional life can also include receiving.

After words have been spoken, there may be value in becoming quiet.

After a mantra has been repeated, there may be value in simply listening.

After scripture has been studied, there may be value in sitting with what has been heard.

The tradition of *śravaṇam* reminds us that spiritual learning begins through receptivity.

We do not deepen only by saying more.

Sometimes we deepen by becoming still enough to receive.

## A Daily Practice of Listening

The teaching of the large ears can be translated into a simple daily practice.

### Before a Conversation

Pause.

Set aside the urge to prove, correct or advise.

Decide that your first task is to understand.

### During the Conversation

Listen without preparing the next sentence.

Notice the words.

Notice the tone.

Notice what seems important to the other person.

Do not interrupt unnecessarily.

### Before Responding

Ask yourself:

**What did I actually hear?**

If necessary, reflect it back in your own words.

Then clarify what remains uncertain.

### After the Conversation

Consider what the encounter taught you.

Did you understand more than you understood before?

Did you notice an assumption of your own?

Did listening change your response?

These questions turn listening into a practice rather than an occasional intention.

## Personal — Listening to Yourself

In personal life, cultivate moments in which you are not immediately consuming information.

Sit quietly.

Write.

Reflect.

Notice recurring patterns.

Allow thoughts and feelings to become visible without instantly judging them.

The goal is not to agree with everything the mind produces.

It is to know what is actually happening within you.

Self-awareness begins with this willingness to listen inwardly.

## Family — Making People Feel Heard

Within the family, make attention visible.

Put the screen away.

Look at the person speaking.

Allow the conversation to finish.

Ask one more question before offering advice.

Do not assume that familiarity eliminates the need for understanding.

A person who feels genuinely heard is more likely to trust, communicate and remain connected.

Listening therefore becomes a form of care.

## Social — Understanding Without Surrendering Conviction

In social life, practise listening to people with whom you disagree.

Do not listen merely to find weaknesses.

Listen to discover the structure of their thinking.

What experiences shaped it?

What values are they protecting?

What are they afraid might be lost?

What do you genuinely share?

Understanding another person's position does not require surrendering your own.

It simply makes disagreement more intelligent.

## Professional — Listening Before Solving

Professionals are often rewarded for solving problems quickly.

Yet premature solutions can be expensive.

Before proposing an answer, understand the problem.

Ask questions.

Listen to different stakeholders.

Distinguish symptoms from causes.

Identify what has already been tried.

Then act.

The discipline of listening first can save enormous effort later.

## Spiritual — Hearing the Deeper Teaching

In spiritual life, let listening become a form of *sādhana*.

Hear sacred teachings.

Reflect upon them.

Return to them.

Notice what changes as your understanding matures.

Listen to mantra.

Listen to silence.

Listen to the movement of your own mind.

The large ears of Gaṇeśa can thus become a reminder that the spiritual path is not only a path of seeking.

It is also a path of receiving.

## From Hearing to Understanding

Listening is only the beginning.

Hearing becomes meaningful when it changes understanding.

Understanding becomes meaningful when it changes behaviour.

And behaviour becomes meaningful when it expresses the values we claim to hold.

The journey can therefore be understood as:

**Hear → Understand → Reflect → Respond → Learn**

This is closely related to the broader movement of the portal itself:

**See → Understand → Contemplate → Practise**

The symbol begins as an image.

It becomes a teaching.

The teaching becomes a practice.

And the practice becomes part of life.

## The Gift of Being Heard

To listen deeply is to give another person something precious.

Attention.

Time.

Respect.

Space.

We live in a world in which people often compete to be seen and heard.

The person who chooses instead to become a place where others can speak safely offers a rare kind of generosity.

This is why the large ears of Gaṇeśa are not merely a symbol of sensory perception.

They can be understood as a symbol of **presence offered to others**.

## The Living Symbol

The large ears remain powerful because their teaching is universal.

Every relationship requires listening.

Every serious learning process requires listening.

Every mature form of leadership requires listening.

Every meaningful spiritual journey requires receptivity.

And every attempt to understand oneself requires inner listening.

The symbol is simple.

Its implications are not.

The ears ask us to become quieter without becoming passive, more receptive without becoming indiscriminate, more patient without becoming hesitant, and more open without losing discernment.

## An Invitation to Practise

For the next conversation that matters, try something simple.

Listen a little longer than usual.

Interrupt a little less.

Ask one genuine question.

Resist the urge to solve immediately.

Notice what changes.

Then try again.

The practice may feel small.

Its effects may not be.

Over time, better listening can change relationships, decisions, learning and leadership.

It can change the quality of our presence.

And, perhaps most importantly, it can change the kind of human being we become.

## The Journey Continues

The large ears of Śrī Gaṇeśa teach us to receive.

But listening alone is not enough.

Once something has been heard, attention must become finer.

The eyes must learn to observe.

The mind must learn to interpret.

And awareness must learn to distinguish what deserves our attention from what merely demands it.

The journey therefore continues from **listening to seeing** — towards the small eyes of Gaṇeśa and the discipline of focused attention.

From speaking less,

to hearing more,

to understanding more deeply,

to living more wisely.

*Śrī Gaṇeśārpaṇam astu।*

*May the large ears of Śrī Gaṇeśa teach us to listen with patience, receive with humility, understand with compassion and respond with wisdom.*
